The AI-Lang project: an introduction
Achilleas Kostoulas (University of Thessaly)

Tere ja tere tulemast! Minu nimi on Achilleas Kostoulas, and I'm deeply honoured to be with you today, even virtually. I'm here in my capacity as the coordinator of the Artificial Intelligence for Language Education project, or AI Lang, of the European Centre for Modern Languages of the Council of Europe. I'm speaking on behalf of a team that also includes Anne-Laure Dubrac, Aleksandra Ljalikova and Elisabeth Pölzleitner, as well as Jérémie Séror, who represents our partner institution, the Official Languages and Bilingualism Institute in Canada.

So over the next 15 minutes or so, I will introduce what we have built, how teachers can use it, and where we're going next.

Now you may reasonably ask, do teachers need yet another set of regulations about how to use artificial intelligence? After all, there is already plenty of very good guidance from the OECD, UNESCO, the Council of Europe and the European Union, including most recently the EU AI Act. Our project builds on this very good guidance but also aims to take it one step further by focusing specifically on language education. And we believe that there is a need to focus on language education for a number of reasons.

Firstly, when we teach second or foreign or additional languages, language becomes both the content of instruction and the medium through which AI operates. Confident, fluent output that is nevertheless wrong or inappropriate is always very hard to detect, and it becomes even more so when we are operating in a language that we have not yet fully mastered.

Secondly, in language education, the struggle to find the right word, the effort to rephrase a sentence or draft a text, all these are the space in which learning takes place. At the same time, they are the steps that AI tends to facilitate, and there is a danger that if we are not very careful, removing the effort might actually remove the learning.

Thirdly, the models on which AI draws have been trained on a small number of dominant languages and standard varieties, whereas less widely spoken languages such as Estonian and regional varieties such as Tartu or Võro, for instance, are less well served. And this happens despite the fact that such varieties are core to the identity and to the voice of the people who use them. So because this kind of linguistic inequality is hardcoded in the AI tools, there is a danger that their uncritical adoption might reproduce social biases. These are the questions that AI Lang invites you to consider.

So what is it then that this project seeks to achieve? Our aim is not to dictate, and it is certainly not to prescribe what teachers ought to do with artificial intelligence. We strongly believe that teachers are experts in their own classrooms, and that top-down guidance, even when it is well-intentioned, can easily become yet another box to tick in the teachers' busy lives, or even an obstacle for them to overcome. So we see it as our job to facilitate a community of practice: language educators who think about AI together, who share what they learn, and who build a common vocabulary for doing so.

To support this community, our project offers three things. First, the website, which is the entry point for everything else. Secondly, a Moodle course for teacher development. And thirdly, a structured framework of guidelines discussing the ethical use of artificial intelligence in language education.

Let me begin with the guidelines. The document on screen consists of three parts. The first part provides the readers with some important background information on the use of artificial intelligence in language education. So this is where you will find definitions, an overview of types of AI, as well as a survey of existing guidance. Part two is the framework itself, and I will walk you through this in a moment. But if you would like to pause and scan the QR code, this is where you will find an interactive version of the guidelines for you to explore. And finally, part three contains usage cases, that is, everyday classroom scenarios, all of them accompanied by some commentary based on the guidance that has been already presented.

The framework itself consists of four principles, and each one of them is operationalised in two guidelines.

The first principle is that the use of AI in language education should be safe. It asks of us: are our uses of AI legally compliant? Are they transparent, and are they low risk? And this is especially important in language education, where a lot of our work involves voice recordings, personal narratives, writing samples, and of course many of our learners are minors.

The second principle is that AI-assisted language education should be responsible. It asks of us whether our teaching practice is a force for positive social change.

Thirdly, we ask that the use of artificial intelligence in language education is purposeful. How does AI add true pedagogical value to what we are doing? Or, put differently, does the use of AI make possible forms of educational activities that would otherwise have been impossible or highly impractical, or does it just involve repackaging what was already done, except with more efficiency?

And finally, the framework asks that the use of artificial intelligence in language education remains human-centred. It preserves human agency.

Now, of course, there is considerable overlap between these guidelines because, for example, a question about social justice is by definition a question about agency. So, while we present these separately for analytical purposes, in any real decision that you face, you will likely find several of these principles at work.

The principles and guidelines that I presented are broad by design, but the framework has been structured in a way to help you think in more concrete terms. And in this slide we will follow one principle all the way through to show you how this happens. So let's start with the informing principle. The use of AI in language education, we argue, should be purposeful. And from this we take one guideline: that it should expand language learning opportunities. Each guideline is then translated into a number of competences, such as, for instance, "I can design AI-supported activities that transform language learning". And finally the reflection prompts, which are very central to the whole approach. So here the competence is reframed as an open-ended question: to what extent do the activities I design transform language education? And this is not a yes-or-no ability. It's a matter of degree, of course. So to help you think about this, we then have a number of questions, such as: what would be lost if I removed AI from this task entirely? Or: what purposes do these activities serve beyond simple efficiency?

Finally, just a few words about how to use the framework. First of all, this is not meant as a compliance checklist. Obviously, there are no correct answers to the reflection questions that we have provided, and teachers are encouraged to use those competences that make the most sense in their professional settings. The answers that you give as you engage with our questions, as you build on your expertise and locally available knowledge, all these are essentially your code of conduct for using AI.

And secondly, I should stress that this framework has been built to help with sharing responsibility. It is not a tool for offloading blame onto teachers. A lot of the guidance here presupposes some legal and technical knowledge that would be unrealistic to expect teachers to have. So the guidelines here are meant to make salient where such gaps in policy and expertise and infrastructure exist, with the understanding that closing these gaps must be a collective endeavour.

To help you turn all this perhaps abstract guidance into concrete pedagogical practice, we have also created a Moodle course, and I would now like to walk you through it.

The course we have designed runs on Moodle, which we expect most teachers to be familiar with, to some extent at least. And as you can see on the screen, there are two versions, an English and a French language version. We are currently working hard to make sure that these are parallel in structure and identical in content. So I will use the English version to tease what kind of content you might find if you log into the course.

The course, as you will see, has a modular structure, which is intended to help you find the content that is most relevant to your needs and interests. And the first module here is a quick orientation into the course, with instructions on how to use it if you are not familiar with how Moodle works.

Moving on, the first of the three major parts in the course provides readers with the theoretical background they might need about artificial intelligence in language education. So this is where you might find the framework that we talked about earlier, as well as information about AI as a mediation tool, sections on how to prompt efficiently, how to use AI in safe and critical ways, as well as how to leverage AI for your lesson planning. This section is particularly relevant to those of you who might benefit from stronger theoretical grounding for your pedagogical thinking.

The second part is much more practically oriented, and as you can see there are sections here on writing and speaking and reading and listening. There's a section on vocabulary, autonomous learning, and how to use AI to assist with providing feedback. Within each of these modules, you will find lots of down-to-earth practical advice, as well as instructions and videos about how to use specific AI tools that you might come across in your teaching and learning.

Next, the third part of the course is a collection of examples of AI use organised in four categories, with lessons and activities for beginners, intermediate and more advanced learners, as well as a section focusing specifically on CLIL. I should say that this collection would not have been possible without contributions from our wider network of experts and workshop participants, who have shared their ideas for lessons and activities with us, and we are really grateful for their commitment and their intellectual generosity.

Each of these lessons is described in terms of level, objectives and AI tools that are used, and here you will find lots of resources that you can use yourselves or use as examples for activities that you design. Now, I should stress that these activities are not intended as models. They are examples that work well in some contexts. And what we would like to encourage is that as you use them, you also think of them as starting points for reflection guided by the principles. We would like you to ask yourselves for each example: is it safe? Is the use of AI responsible? Is it purposeful? Are the outcomes human-centred? And if you find yourself sceptical about any of these, we would like to invite you to think about how you might adjust this activity to make it work better for your contexts.

Finally, after part three, there is an appendix with additional resources. And this is where you will find several inventories of digital and AI resources that you can use, which are curated by partner projects including ICT-REV at the ECML, the TRAILS project and others. And this is also where you will find our working index of policy documents. This index puts together all the guidance that has been published to date about the use of AI, and you can use this to search for guidance that is relevant to your context.

As you can see, this is quite a substantial learning resource. And one thing I really want to stress is that you do not have to take all of it to benefit from this course. Of course, you can take the whole course from start to finish if this is something you want to do, or you could just visit the sections that you need when you need them. So we would like to invite you to explore all these resources and keep coming back to them whenever your questions change. And we would also like to encourage you to keep creating new materials and sharing ideas, because the whole point of the AI Lang project is not about us having the final word on how AI can be used in language education. It is about starting the conversation that we now hand over to you.

Let's now talk about what happens next. As the project draws towards its conclusion, there are a number of dissemination activities planned, and the first one is a webinar by the European Centre for Modern Languages, which is scheduled to take place on Thursday the 28th of January at 4:00 p.m. Central European Time. We look forward to seeing many of you there. Looking further into the future, we hope to offer a number of training opportunities for teachers and teacher educators. So if you would like to further explore the guidelines and the professional development course together with us, we look forward to hearing from you.

And this brings me to the question of how to get in touch. Everything I talked about is collected in our website, and there you can find information about the guidelines and the link to the Moodle course, news about upcoming activities, as well as our emails, which you can use to get in touch with us. You can also contact us through LinkedIn, where we have a group dedicated to the project.

Before I sign off, I'd like to restate what I believe might be the most important take-home message from this video. There is no doubt that artificial intelligence is transforming the landscape of language education, and the questions that it raises are far too important to be answered by technology firms alone or through policy deliberations outside education. We believe that these are questions that need to be answered by teachers, in dialogue with their learners and in dialogue with each other. We see it as the job of this project to make this dialogue more effective and better informed.

Thank you very much for watching, and we look forward to hearing from you.
