Using artificial intelligence: surveying existing guidance

Considering the rapid development of artificial intelligence, it seems unsurprising that several institutions and organisations worldwide have taken up the call and promulgated guidelines to address the multifaceted challenges posed by AI technologies. This page presents an overview of legislation, institutional guidelines and other advice on the use of AI in educational contexts.

The international landscape

Several attempts to regulate the usage of artificial intelligence have originated from intergovernmental or supranational entities.

  • The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (UNESCO, 2021) presents a comprehensive human rights-based approach. Key tenets include proportionality, safety and security, privacy and data protection, human oversight, transparency, accountability, sustainability and the promotion of multi-stakeholder governance.
  • The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)’s AI Principles focus on fostering innovative and trustworthy AI while upholding respect for human rights and democratic values.
  • The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law, adopted in 2024, represents a milestone in global AI regulation. Some key provisions are that AI systems must respect human rights, the rule of law and democratic values throughout their life cycle; that all parties should assess and, where relevant, mitigate risks caused by the use of AI; that there should be human oversight, transparency and accountability in the use of AI systems; and that AI use promotes safety, non-discrimination and privacy.

Existing legislation (e.g., the General Data Protection Regulation in the European Union, or the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act in Canada), which predates the widespread use of artificial intelligence, remains relevant as well.

The Council of Europe Education Strategy

In parallel with the above, the Council of Europe is actively engaging with the emerging challenges and dilemmas associated with the educational deployment of AI technology, as part of its Education Strategy 2024–2030 (Council of Europe, 2024). While artificial intelligence is not explicitly mentioned as a focus point, the strategy provides a broad frame for its integration into education. This frame highlights dimensions such as the democratic mission of education, social responsibility and a human rights-based approach to digital transformation. The strategy also calls for new legal and policy frames, reference standards and capacity-building tools for the new educational realities that are emerging.

The strategic vision of the Council of Europe is further articulated in the Artificial Intelligence and Education project. One of the main outputs has been a preparatory study for the development of a legal instrument that regulates the use of AI in education (Havinga et al., 2024). Key elements of this policy include (a) the priority of human rights, (b) transparency and oversight, (c) accountability, (d) democratic principles, (e) inclusivity and equitable education, and (f) shared governance mechanisms. This is intended to ensure that the use of artificial intelligence is done within the frame of a principled, robust and flexible regulatory response.

This preparatory work has since culminated in a formal policy instrument, the Council of Europe Recommendation on AI literacy (Council of Europe, 2026). The Recommendation defines AI literacy as “the capacity to engage with AI critically, ethically, responsibly, safely and effectively” along three dimensions: the human, the technological and the practical, with particular emphasis on the first. The Recommendation is articulated in the form of guiding principles, several of which bear directly on language education. Some of the most important are: that AI is sociotechnical rather than neutral; that AI systems may threaten diversity by reinforcing cultural and linguistic biases and standardisation; that human agency, judgment or responsibility must be preserved rather than delegated to automated systems; and that individuals should be enabled to make informed decisions about their use or non-use of AI. Because it is addressed to member states rather than to individual language teachers, the Recommendation stops short of specifying what AI literacy might look like in a language classroom.
