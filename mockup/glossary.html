Glossary

Agency

Agency for language learners and teachers refers to their capacity to act purposefully and remain in control of learning and teaching practices, respectively. In the context of using AI for language education, agency involves making informed choices about which aspects of language education are delegated to artificial intelligence and ensuring that learning experiences are consistently guided by human judgment and values.

  • Moore J. W. (2016), “What is the sense of agency and why does it matter?”, Frontiers in Psychology, available at www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5002400/, accessed 4 July 2024
  • Godwin-Jones R. (2024), “Distributed agency in second language learning and teaching through generative AI”, Language Learning & Technology, Vol. 28, Issue 2, pp. 5-31, available at: https://doi.org/10.64152/10125/73570.

Agentic artificial intelligence

Agentic artificial intelligence is a type of AI that can pursue defined goals autonomously across multiple steps, taking actions and making decisions with minimal human intervention. In language education, agentic AI might be used to design and deliver sequences of learning activities, monitor learner progress, and adapt instruction accordingly.

  • Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) (2026b), The agentic AI landscape and its conceptual foundations, OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 56, OECD Publishing, Paris, available at: https://doi.org/10.1787/396cf758-en.

Artificial intelligence in language education

Artificial intelligence in language education refers to the ability of technology to undertake, with varying degrees of autonomy, assistive tasks that are normally subsumed into the roles of teachers, for the purposes of improving language learning. Such tasks include, for instance, lesson planning, materials preparation, providing opportunities for practice, and supporting assessment.

  • Goh C. C. M. and Aryadoust V. (2025), “Developing and assessing second language listening and speaking: does AI make it better?”, Annual Review of Applied Linguistics, Vol. 45, pp. 179-199, available at: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0267190525100111.
  • Wiboolyasarin W. et al. (2025), “AI-driven chatbots in second language education: a systematic review of their efficacy and pedagogical implications”, Ampersand, Vol. 14, Article 100224, available at: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amper.2025.100224.

Big data

Big data are datasets which cannot be processed or analysed using conventional data management tools, because of their volume, speed of generation, and variety (e.g., text, audio, video, or metadata). In language education, big data include – among other things – information about learner proficiency and achievement, personal profiles, and interaction logs from digital platforms, all of which might be used to generate personalised learning experiences or to inform educational policy.

  • Williamson B. (2017), Big data in education: the digital future of learning, policy and practice, SAGE, London.

Classroom-based inquiry

Classroom-based inquiry refers to a reflective and systematic set of practices that aim to identify how teaching can be improved, collecting and analysing data in the process of teaching, and using them to develop actionable insights. Classroom-based inquiry differs from formal research in that it prioritises teacher knowledge and because it is shaped by the particularities of the classroom under investigation.

  • Kostoulas A. (2026), “Classroom-based research in language education”, in Nesi H. and Milin P. (eds), International encyclopedia of language and linguistics (3rd edn, Vol. 9. pp. 840-844), Elsevier, Amsterdam, available at: https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-323-95504-1.01299-0.

Conversational agents

Conversational agents (also known as chatbots or, outside language education, as assistants) are AI applications that simulate real-time dialogue with language learners. In language teaching and learning, conversational agents can help learners to practise speaking or writing in a target language, possibly also providing focused feedback on their output.

  • Bibauw S., François T. and Desmet P. (2019), “Discussing with a computer to practice a foreign language: research synthesis and conceptual framework of dialogue-based CALL”, Computer Assisted Language Learning, Vol. 32, Issue 8, pp. 827-877, available at: https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2018.1535508.

Critical use of AI

Critical use of AI, in the context of these guidelines, refers to usages of AI that promote social justice. This definition of “criticality” is associated with: (1) an awareness of power structures, as opposed to treating AI as a ‘neutral’ tool, (2) a normative orientation invested in the expansion of democratic agency, (3) reflexivity, which involves awareness of the role of teachers and learners in the interaction with technology, and (4) a transformative intent. This definition differs from other uses of the word “critical”, which foreground evaluation and fault-finding or higher-order cognition.

  • Pennycook A. (2021), Critical applied linguistics: a critical re-introduction (2nd edn), Routledge, London.

Data mining

Data mining is the automated analysis of large sets of learner input (e.g., learner profiles, voice recordings, writing samples) or metadata (e.g., engagement metrics, device information, location) in order to identify patterns. Such data mining can take place in order to personalise learning experiences, track progress, or generate learner profiles, but it can also be used for optimising applications or – more problematically – for commercial purposes.

  • Liu Q. and Khalil M. (2023), “Understanding privacy and data protection issues in learning analytics using a systematic review”, British Journal of Educational Technology, Vol. 54, Issue 6, pp. 1338-1356, available at: https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13388.
  • Romero C. and Ventura S. (2020), “Educational data mining and learning analytics: an updated survey”, WIREs Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery, Vol. 10, Issue 3, Article e1355, available at: https://doi.org/10.1002/widm.1355.

Generative artificial intelligence

Generative artificial intelligence is a type of AI that can generate new text or audio (or, less frequently in language education, images and code), from information in a large dataset. Some ways that generative AI applications have been used in language education include lesson plan generation, text adaptation, dialogue creation, or the production of language practice scenarios. Large language models, such as ChatGPT, are types of generative AI.

  • Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) (2026a), OECD digital education outlook 2026: exploring effective uses of generative AI in education, OECD Publishing, Paris, available at: https://doi.org/10.1787/062a7394-en.

Hallucinations

Hallucinations, in the context of artificial intelligence, are instances where an AI system produces output that is fluent and plausible but factually inaccurate, or even completely fabricated.

  • Ji Z. et al. (2023), “Survey of hallucination in natural language generation”, ACM Computing Surveys, Vol. 55, Issue 12, Article 248, pp. 1-38, available at: https://doi.org/10.1145/3571730.

Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITSs)

Intelligent tutoring systems (ITSs) are applications of AI that provide learners with personalised instruction and feedback, and adaptive opportunities for language practice. A strength of ITSs is that they analyse the learners’ performance in real time, and can adjust the type, sequencing and difficulty of language learning tasks based on the learners’ strengths, goals, and interests.

  • VanLehn K. (2011), “The relative effectiveness of human tutoring, intelligent tutoring systems, and other tutoring systems”, Educational Psychologist, Vol. 46, Issue 4, pp. 197-221, available at: https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369.

Large Language Models (LLMs)

Large Language Models (LLMs) are models trained on very large text corpora, which can be used to generate human-like language by predicting the most probable next word in a sequence. This makes them a subset of generative AI, with a focus on text. By analysing statistical patterns in language, LLMs can produce text that is coherent and contextually appropriate but is not based on actual ‘understanding’. In language education, LLMs can be used to support conversational practice, scaffold writing, or generate learning materials.

  • Bender E. M. et al. (2021), “On the dangers of stochastic parrots: can language models be too big?”, in Proceedings of the 2021 ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT ’21), pp. 610-623, Association for Computing Machinery, New York, available at: https://doi.org/10.1145/3442188.3445922.
  • Navigli R., Conia S. and Ross B. (2023), “Biases in large language models: origins, inventory, and discussion”, ACM Journal of Data and Information Quality, Vol. 15, Issue 2, pp. 1-21.

Metadata

Metadata are information that describes or accompanies interaction with an AI application. Such information might include the time and location of an interaction, device type, browser settings, or engagement metrics (e.g., time spent on a task, click patterns).

  • Riley J. (2017), Understanding metadata: what is metadata, and what is it for? A primer, National Information Standards Organization (NISO), available at: https://www.niso.org/publications/understanding-metadata-2017.

Predictive artificial intelligence

Predictive artificial intelligence is a type of artificial intelligence that looks for patterns in data to predict outcomes or suggest next steps. In language education, predictive AI can be used to help identify learners’ strengths and weaknesses or to recommend personalised learning pathways.

  • Romero C. and Ventura S. (2020), “Educational data mining and learning analytics: an updated survey”, WIREs Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery, Vol. 10, Issue 3, Article e1355, available at: https://doi.org/10.1002/widm.1355.

Profiling

Profiling involves the use of learner responses or metadata to create personal learner profiles. Such profiles can be used to generate individualised learning pathways or adaptive feedback, but teachers and learners should be aware of possible risks such as reinforcing bias, narrowing opportunities, and the use of incomplete or context-insensitive data.

  • European Parliament and Council of the European Union (2016), Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 April 2016 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (General Data Protection Regulation), available at: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj, accessed 12 October 2026.

Prompt

A prompt is a question, set of instructions or similar input that teachers and learners provide to AI systems in order to elicit explanations, create texts, simulate conversations, or adapt materials.

  • Park J. and Choo S. (2025), “Generative AI prompt engineering for educators: practical strategies”, Journal of Special Education Technology, Vol. 40, Issue 3, pp. 411-417, available at: https://doi.org/10.1177/01626434241298954.

Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge

Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge, or TPACK, is a framework of knowledge described by Mishra and Koehler (2006), which positions effective technology integration as emerging from the intersection of three knowledge domains: (1) content knowledge, which encompasses subject matter, including linguistic, metalinguistic and cultural knowledge in the case of language education, (2) pedagogical knowledge, which involves broad knowledge of educational theory, learning processes, and curriculum design, and (3) technological knowledge, which involves understanding of and facility with digital tools and emerging technologies.

  • Mishra P. and Koehler M. J. (2006), “Technological pedagogical content knowledge: a framework for teacher knowledge”, Teachers College Record, Vol. 108, Issue 6, pp. 1017-1054, available at: https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9620.2006.00684.x.
