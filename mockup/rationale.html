Situating AI in language education: exploring what it can offer

Although the use of digital technology to support language learning predates the emergence of AI (e.g., computer-assisted language learning, or CALL, developed as a sub-field of language education as early as the 1980s; e.g., Guichon, 2024), the rapid spread of artificial intelligence and the speed with which it has permeated education have precipitated a visible qualitative change that necessitates rethinking how to best approach instruction, assessment and learner support.

Rethinking language

Reassessing how instruction and learning are approached means revisiting what counts as language use itself. Literacy, in an era of artificial intelligence, is likely to extend to the ability to interpret, mediate and generate meaning across diverse media (cf. Rowsell & Walsh, 2011), as opposed to narrower definitions of language learning, which confined themselves to developing communicative skills.

This shift is usefully framed by the New London Group’s (1996) account of multiliteracies, which placed design at the centre of literate activity, and which Kalantzis and Cope (2025) have since revisited in the light of generative AI. In this theoretical account, learners draw on “available designs” (i.e., existing conventions and discourses), transforming them into “redesigned” ones. AI output, a fluent aggregation of existing conventions, functions as an available design, which learners need to work through meaning-making practices such as prompting, interrogating and re-voicing. Such meaning-making involves engaging with language in diverse technologised forms, including skills for reading, interpreting, reflecting on, mediating and producing texts in multiple modalities (Chun et al., 2016). This constitutes a foundation for the development of pluriliteracies needed for deeper learning in different languages and cultures (Coyle & Meyer, 2021; Meyer et al., 2026).

The case for artificial intelligence in language education

Seen against this backdrop of rapid technological change, evidence suggests that artificial intelligence can support language teaching and learning in important ways, provided it is thoughtfully deployed.

For example, a recent systematic review of the efficacy and pedagogical implications of AI-driven chatbots in second language education (Wiboolyasarin et al., 2025) provides insights into how AI-assisted tools can support teachers in tasks such as providing personalised feedback, differentiated instruction and adaptive learning (Godwin-Jones, 2024). More broadly, artificial intelligence can enhance the language learning experience by enabling individualised learning paths, generating timely, individualised feedback and scaffolding in real time, extending access to language support beyond the classroom, and supporting repeated, low-stakes practice. Additionally, AI has been shown to foster increased learner engagement (e.g., Liu & Reinders, 2025; Yuan & Liu, 2025), a key predictor of success in language learning.

In more practical terms, AI tools can also serve as pre-writing brainstorming partners, virtual conversation simulators to develop speaking skills, including fluency, coherence, lexical and grammatical accuracy and phonological range, as well as to promote learners’ willingness to communicate (Fathi et al., 2024; Hapsari & Wu, 2022). Additionally, they can function as reading companions, supporting learners in self-regulated, paced reading (Shafiee Rad, 2025). For learners with less developed proficiency in the target language, AI use has been associated with stronger writing self-efficacy (Warschauer et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025), and with support across multiple stages of academic writing, including outlining, revising, proofreading and structured reflection (Su et al., 2023).

Artificial intelligence applications can also improve the quality of linguistic input to which learners are exposed. In some teaching contexts, educators may have limited access to up-to-date, varied and contextually rich linguistic resources for developing learning materials, including instructions, model texts and exercises. In such cases, AI tools can support materials development by expanding access to grammatically accurate, pragmatically appropriate and communicatively authentic language examples that complement teacher expertise. Similarly, in contexts where teachers lack confidence or expertise in generating communicative and task-based learning activities, artificial intelligence can empower teachers to move beyond overused (and often questionable) instructional formats, such as repetition, gap-fills or cloze activities, thus enabling learners to engage in more varied communicative and learner-centred alternatives (Wang & Tian, 2025).
