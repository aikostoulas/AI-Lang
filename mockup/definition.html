Towards a definition of artificial intelligence in language education

I propose to consider the question, ‘Can machines think?’ This should begin with definitions of the meaning of the terms ‘machine’ and ‘think’.

Turing (1950, §1, p. 433)

Applications of artificial intelligence are rapidly becoming embedded in all aspects of life, from professional and scientific domains to personal interests and leisure activities. Even when we focus narrowly on education, or even more narrowly on language teaching and learning, AI applications can provide support for a wide range of activities (Goh & Aryadoust, 2025), including conversational practice, text adaptation and translation, lesson planning, listening practice, communicative and form-focused tasks, reading comprehension, support with metalinguistic language explanations as well as assistance with assessment and feedback, and more.

Some definitions of artificial intelligence

One implication of the above is that, despite its widespread availability and apparent familiarity, AI resists a single settled definition. The instruments that currently shape policy and practice offer the following:

  • UNESCO

    AI systems are information-processing technologies that integrate models and algorithms that produce a capacity to learn and to perform cognitive tasks, leading to outcomes such as prediction and decision making in material and virtual environments [operating] with varying degrees of autonomy by means of knowledge modelling and representation and by exploiting data and calculating correlations. (UNESCO, 2021, para. 2(a))

  • OECD

    An AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from the input it receives how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. Different AI systems vary in their levels of autonomy and adaptiveness after deployment. (OECD, 2024)

  • European Union

    ‘AI system’ means a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2024, Art. 3(1))

  • Council of Europe: Framework Convention

    ‘Artificial intelligence system’ means a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that may influence physical or virtual environments. (Council of Europe, 2024a, Art. 2)

  • Council of Europe: Recommendation on AI literacy

    ‘Artificial intelligence’ (AI) encompasses a growing range of sociotechnical systems that: (i) comprise the interplay of people, institutions, policies, social practices and the environment, on the one hand, and computational devices, infrastructure, software, data and statistics, on the other, which together shape the design, development, deployment and use of such systems; and (ii) are designed to automate, augment or replace actions or tasks normally associated with human intelligence. (Council of Europe, 2026, para. 7.a)

Common ground

The proliferation of definitional attempts is, in part, caused by the attempt to highlight different aspects of AI (as a system and as a field), depending on the needs and mission of various organisations. While such polyphony results in discourse about AI that is broader than it is precise, juxtaposing these definitions can reveal substantial common ground, which can be summarised in the following statements:

  • AI systems are machine-based and operate by inferring outputs (predictions, content, recommendations or decisions) from input (Council of Europe, 2024a; European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2024; OECD, 2024).
  • AI systems have various degrees of autonomy (Council of Europe, 2024a; European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2024; OECD, 2024; UNESCO, 2021).
  • AI systems are designed to automate, augment or replace tasks normally associated with human intelligence (Council of Europe, 2026, para. 7.a.ii; UNESCO, 2021, para. 2(a)).
  • AI systems are sociotechnical rather than purely technical, in the sense that they reflect the design choices and assumptions of their developers, and the dominant values of the contexts in which they are created (Council of Europe, 2026, paras. 7.a.i and 9).

What is artificial intelligence for language education?

Building on the observations above, and thinking specifically about the use of artificial intelligence in language teaching and learning, we define AI for language education as follows:

Artificial intelligence for language education

Artificial intelligence for language education refers to the use of AI-enabled systems and applications to mediate, support, augment or partially automate language teaching and learning processes, including teacher practices (for example, planning, materials design, feedback and assessment support) and learner practice (for example, rehearsal, interaction, drafting and revising, comprehension support and self-regulation), all with the aim of improving language learning conditions and outcomes, whether in teacher-guided instructional settings or self-directed learner contexts, and always in alignment with ethical governance principles.
